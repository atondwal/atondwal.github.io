HOW to BECOME a GOOD DEEP LEARNING RESEARCHER

With a focus on mechanistic interpretability and alignment

Inspired by Gerard 't Hooft's guide for theoretical physicists

This is a guide for those who are determined to understand deep learning deeply - not just to use it, but to understand why it works, to peer inside neural networks and understand their mechanisms, and to work on making AI systems safe and aligned with human values. If you really want to contribute to our understanding of deep learning systems - and it is an exciting experience if you succeed! - there are many things you need to know.

You don't have to believe anything you read on faith - check it. Try alternative approaches, as many as you can. Implement things yourself. You will discover, occasionally, that those who came before often did the smartest thing possible. Amazing. Try to outsmart the authors.

The best resources come with exercises. Do them. Better yet, invent your own exercises. Implement papers from scratch. Try to reach the stage that you discover errors in papers, and imagine how you would do it better.

Don't expect this to be quick. You won't get your breakthrough for free, and remember, all of this together takes dedicated students at least 2-3 years of intense study. More than rudimentary intelligence is assumed to be present, because ordinary students can master this material only when assisted by patient teachers.

Deep learning is like a skyscraper. It has solid foundations in mathematics, classical machine learning, and fundamental deep learning concepts. Don't think that classical ML is "irrelevant" since now we have transformers. In those days, the solid foundations were laid of the knowledge that we enjoy now. Don't try to construct your skyscraper without first reconstructing these foundations yourself.

We favor the physicist's approach: not pure theorem-proof rigor, not cookbook "plug and chug", but derivation → intuition → application. Understand why the math works. Derive things yourself. Feel it in your bones.

The choice of topics and the amount of ink spilled on them represent my obvious biases. Take heed. YMMV.

Layer 0: Engineering Prerequisites

Before you can do anything, you need to be able to code. This is not core to the theory, but you cannot do experiments without it.

DS&A / Algorithmic Thinking:
Neetcode 150, LeetCode

Practical Tooling:
MIT Missing Semester
  • Data Structures & Algorithms: Big-O (why is attention O(n²)?), graphs, hashmaps, DP.
  • Practical Tooling: Shell, git, debugging, profiling. git bisect can save you weeks.

Framework fluency (PyTorch, JAX, Einops) comes from use. These help you get started: UvA PyTorch, JAX autodidax, Einops. But you'll really learn by implementing the later layers.

Before moving on: you can use git to clone, branch, and resolve a merge conflict; you understand Big-O; you can run and modify tutorials. You can come back for the rest as you need it.

Layer 3: Deep Learning Fundamentals

The core of deep learning. You must be able to derive backprop through any operation by hand. You must understand why architectures are shaped the way they are.

Backprop Deep Dive:

Optimization:

Papers to Implement:
BatchNorm, LayerNorm, ResNet papers

Intuition (no exercises):
3B1B Neural Networks

This layer breaks into five essential sub-topics:

3-i: Backprop & Autodiff

Non-negotiable. You must be able to derive backprop through any operation by hand.

  • Build micrograd - implement autograd in ~100 lines
  • Understand computational graphs, what .backward() actually does
  • Work through backprop on paper for: softmax, cross-entropy, batch norm, attention
  • Read the CS231n backprop notes carefully

3-ii: Architectures

Why these structures work

  • MLPs: Universal approximation theorem - what does it actually say? What are the limits?
  • Depth vs width: Why do we prefer deep over wide? What can deep nets represent that shallow ones can't?
  • Build makemore - understand embeddings, lead into transformers

3-iii: Optimization Dynamics

  • SGD: Why noise helps (implicit regularization, escaping saddle points)
  • Momentum, Adam: Derive them. Understand as approximating second-order information.
  • Loss landscapes: The geometry of neural net optimization. Why aren't local minima as bad as we feared?
  • Learning rates: Warmup, schedules. Not just "use cosine" but why.

3-iv: Generalization & Regularization

  • Classical view: Bias-variance tradeoff, overfitting, underfitting
  • Modern puzzle: Why do overparameterized nets generalize? Double descent.
  • Regularization techniques: Dropout (derive it as approximate Bayesian inference), weight decay, data augmentation

3-v: Training Dynamics

  • Initialization: Xavier/He - derive why these scalings are necessary
  • Normalization: BatchNorm, LayerNorm - what problem do they solve? Derive the backward pass.
  • Residual connections: Why do they help? Gradient flow perspective, ensemble interpretation.

The cardinal sin this prevents: People who can import transformers but can't explain why LayerNorm is where it is, or what the backward pass through softmax looks like.

Before moving on: derive backprop through softmax and batch norm on paper, implement micrograd, explain why ResNets train better than plain deep nets.

Infrastructure & Compute

Needed to run experiments at scale. Not required.

GPU Programming:
CUDA C Programming Guide
NVIDIA CUDA Intro

Profiling & Debugging:
PyTorch Profiler
NVIDIA Nsight
  • GPU programming basics: Understanding CUDA, kernels, memory hierarchy. You don't need to write CUDA kernels daily, but understanding what's happening under the hood helps you debug performance issues.
  • Cluster usage: SLURM, job scheduling, multi-node training. How to not be the person who hogs the cluster.
  • Distributed training: Data parallelism, model parallelism, pipeline parallelism. When do you need each?
  • Profiling: Finding bottlenecks. Is it compute-bound or memory-bound? How to read profiler output.
  • Debugging at scale: When your training run dies at hour 47, how do you figure out why?
  • Mixed precision: FP16, BF16, when and why to use them.

Vision

CNNs and computer vision. Even if your focus is language models, the concepts here (translation equivariance, hierarchical features, weight sharing) are fundamental.

Primary:
CS231n (Stanford)
CS231n Course Notes - Excellent written materials

Key Notes:
Convolutional Networks
Visualizing CNNs
  • Convolutions: Derive backprop through a conv layer by hand. Understand as weight sharing.
  • Translation equivariance: Why convolutions give you this for free. What other equivariances might you want?
  • Hierarchical features: Edges → textures → parts → objects. Receptive fields.
  • Pooling, striding: What they do to translation equivariance. Tradeoffs.
  • Classic architectures: LeNet, AlexNet, VGG, ResNet - understand the progression of ideas
  • Visualizations: What do intermediate layers see? Activation maximization, feature visualization.
